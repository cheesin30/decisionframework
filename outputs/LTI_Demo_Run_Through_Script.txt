LTI MATERIALS RUN-THROUGH — THOUGHT-PROCESS SCRIPT
============================================================

Companion to: LTI_Presentation_Script.txt
Starts at:    Slide 6 — "Now, the flow in action."
Runtime:      ~8-10 minutes
Flow:         PREPARE -> DELIVER -> FOLLOW UP

Principle for this section: the screen shows WHAT it is —
you only ever say WHY it's built that way. Never read the
material aloud. Open it, give the room two seconds to look,
then explain the decision behind it.

SETUP CHECKLIST (before the session)
  [ ] Pre-Workshop Diagnostic + Post-Workshop Survey open in tabs
  [ ] The four toolkit PDFs open in order
  [ ] Engagement Calendar tool open
  [ ] QR tested with your own phone that morning
  [ ] Printed toolkit copies to pass around (optional but strong)


------------------------------------------------------------
PART 1 — PREPARE  ·  the pre-workshop survey
------------------------------------------------------------
(~2 minutes. Open the diagnostic. Pause. Then talk about
decisions, not questions.)

OPEN the Pre-Workshop Diagnostic. Let them read the cover. Then:

  The first design decision was where the experience starts. Not
  in the room — before it. If an adviser's first contact with
  this material is the moment they sit down, they arrive as a
  spectator. I wanted them to arrive having already named their
  own problem.

  Second decision: everything is tap, not type. The moment you
  ask an adviser to write sentences, completion collapses. Two
  minutes, phone-friendly, done in the lift on the way up.

SCROLL slowly through the questions while you talk:

  Third — and this is the one I care most about — every answer
  option is plausible. There's no obviously "correct" choice to
  perform. That matters because this looks like a survey, but
  it's really a diagnostic: one of these questions quietly tests
  whether an adviser's instinct is to respond to a client concern
  with data, or to first ask what's driving it. If the right
  answer were obvious, everyone would pick it and I'd learn
  nothing. Because all four feel reasonable, people answer
  honestly — and the honest answers tell me exactly what the
  room needs.

  Last decision here: I end with a confidence self-rating, and I
  deliberately ask the identical questions again after the
  workshop. One survey tells you opinions. Two identical
  measurements tell you whether anything changed. That
  before-and-after is the evidence this whole experience stands on.

TRANSITION:
  "So that's the front door. Now the part I rebuilt the most —
  what advisers actually leave with."


------------------------------------------------------------
PART 2 — DELIVER  ·  the advisor toolkit (4 pieces)
------------------------------------------------------------
(~4 minutes. One decision-story per document. Open each,
two-second pause, then the why.)

--- 2a. CLIENT CONVERSATION TIP SHEET (~1 min) ---

OPEN it. Pause. Then:

  The constraint I set myself here was brutal: one page. Not
  because paper is expensive — because a second page is where
  method goes to die. If an adviser can't find the response
  while the client is still talking, the material has failed.

  The structure came from a real observation: advisers don't
  struggle with knowledge, they struggle in the moment. So it's
  organised by what the client SAYS — the objection in the
  client's own words — not by investment topic. You look up the
  sentence you just heard, not a chapter.

  And I wrote the response lines word-for-word on purpose. Not
  because advisers can't write their own — but because in a live
  conversation, having A line beats composing THE line. They'll
  make it their own by week two. The sheet just gets them through
  conversation one.

--- 2b. ADVISOR TALKING POINTS (~1 min) ---

OPEN it, flick two pages. Then:

  These charts existed before I touched anything — they're the
  strongest part of the workshop. The problem was their shape: a
  chart in a deck is something you present AT someone. I wanted
  something an adviser can USE.

  So the design rule for every page is the same triple: three
  things you can say, one question you ask the client, one thing
  YOU do next. Say, ask, do. The chart earns its place only if it
  converts into a move in a conversation.

  The "ask your client" line is the one I fought for hardest —
  because the diagnostic showed most advisers respond to concerns
  with information. Every page here forces the opposite habit:
  the chart sets up a question, not a lecture.

--- 2c. CLIENT BOOKLET (~1 min) ---

OPEN the cover. Then:

  Everything else you've seen is for the adviser. This one exists
  because at some point the adviser needs something to physically
  put in a client's hands — and nothing in the workshop was
  client-safe. Different audience, different rules: no jargon, no
  framework language, nothing to study. Four ideas, one per
  spread, each anchored on a single number, because clients
  remember one number per idea and nothing more.

  The ending was a deliberate choice: it doesn't end with a
  conclusion, it ends with three questions the client can only
  really answer WITH their adviser. A brochure that ends with
  answers gets filed. One that ends with questions books the next
  meeting. That's the whole trick of it.

--- 2d. STEPS FOR SUCCESS (~1 min) ---

OPEN it. Then:

  This piece exists because of the most predictable failure in
  all workshop training: everything is forgotten by Friday. I
  didn't want more content — I wanted a behaviour to survive
  contact with a busy diary.

  So it's built like a habit programme, not a curriculum: one
  action per touchpoint, sized at three minutes, over eight
  weeks. Each action is one rep of the method in a REAL client
  conversation — because the workshop can only ever rehearse;
  the habit forms in the field.

  And notice it assumes nobody re-reads instructions: the
  sequence is designed to arrive as calendar reminders. Which is
  the perfect segue —

TRANSITION:
  "— because the honest question about all of this is: who
  actually remembers to send the right thing at the right time?
  Nobody. So I made 'nobody' the design requirement."


------------------------------------------------------------
PART 3 — FOLLOW UP  ·  the engagement calendar (+ the scan)
------------------------------------------------------------
(~3 minutes. Open the tool. This is the peak — slow down.)

OPEN the touchpoint view. Then:

  Two decisions define this tool. The first is how FEW
  touchpoints there are. My first draft had twelve — it felt
  thorough, and it was wrong. Twelve reminders is noise; noise
  gets muted; muted means zero. I cut it to three, and the test
  for what survived was simple: each touchpoint had to carry the
  entire method in one action. Day zero, one real concern. Week
  one, one full response. Month one, one kept habit. Iteration
  here wasn't adding — it was deleting.

  The second decision: the follow-up lives in the adviser's OWN
  calendar, not in another portal or newsletter. Emails get
  archived; portals need remembering. A calendar reminder is the
  one notification that interrupts a working day at the moment
  it's meant to.

SHOW the export briefly:

  And it's built once, exported forever — a consultant doesn't
  assemble follow-ups per workshop; the journey with all its
  materials travels as a single file.

NOW the QR. Put it on screen:

  The last problem was delivery, and it's harder than it looks:
  how do you get a calendar file with attachments onto a
  stranger's phone in a workshop, with no login, in seconds? My
  first attempts failed in interesting ways — a shared link that
  iPhones refused to trust taught me that the QR can only ever
  point somewhere, never carry the file.

  The version that works is deliberately boring: the code opens a
  short form — name and email, nothing else — and an automated
  flow inside our own Microsoft environment emails the calendar
  out. About thirty seconds, no external hosting, nothing for IT
  to worry about.

  Try it — grab your phones and scan.

WHILE PHONES ARE OUT (one sentence only):

  One design principle in that half-minute of waiting: I removed
  a sign-in step from an earlier version — every extra field
  costs participation, and I'd rather have engagement than data.

WHEN PHONES BUZZ: stop talking. Let it land. Then:

  What you just experienced is the adviser's last thirty seconds
  of the workshop — and the reason the learning doesn't end when
  the room empties.

CLOSE THE LOOP (optional, 20 seconds):

  And the post-workshop survey re-measures the same confidence
  baseline from the start — so every workshop produces its own
  before-and-after, and tells us what to fix next time.

TRANSITION (back to the deck, Slide 7):
  "So that's the thinking, end to end. One more thing — where
  this goes next."

------------------------------------------------------------
IF SOMETHING FAILS — FALLBACKS
------------------------------------------------------------

  QR won't scan on the room screen
    -> read out the short link, or show the email arriving on
       YOUR phone via the room screen.

  Email takes longer than 30 seconds
    -> keep going; circle back when the first phone buzzes:
       "there it is."

  No internet / Forms down
    -> the printed toolkit carries Part 2; talk Parts 1 and 3
       over static screenshots kept in the deck's appendix.
